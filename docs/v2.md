# Lantern v2: the design

**Status: designed here; built and tested on a separate local branch, where an adversarial review found eight issues, one of them high, all fixed; not deployed, and not part of this submission.** The shipped contract on Preprod is frozen and unchanged.

The shipped contract, `contracts/src/lantern.compact`, cannot change. Every recorded deployment replaced its maintenance authority with an empty committee, so no one can change a deployed instance's rules, and `npm run devnet:verify` checks each verifier key on the chain against a fresh compile of that exact source. The shipped recovery on Preprod, which can finalize from 27 September 2026, 15:03 UTC, needs proofs from those same keys. So every change that needs the contract lives in a new contract, `lantern2.compact`, on a branch of its own. It imports [`identity.compact`](../contracts/src/identity.compact) and [`ownergate.compact`](../contracts/src/ownergate.compact) unchanged, so an identity commitment and the "current owner" rule mean the same thing in both. The v1 contract, its records, the story and the site stay byte-identical.

This page is the design in brief: why each change, the rule, the privacy argument and the cost. The row counts below are the branch's measurements of the built contract after its review ([the circuits, measured](#the-circuits-measured)), with compact 0.31.1, and all 13 of its circuits fit k ≤ 14. Until v2 is merged, everything here is a design as far as this repository goes, and neither the README nor SECURITY.md claims any of it for the shipped contract.

## Where these come from

In September 2026 we compared Lantern with these recovery products, from our reading of each one's public documentation: Argent, Safe with Candide and RecoveryHub, zkEmail, Soul, Loopring, Coinbase, Privy, ZenGo, zkLogin, Casa, Nunchuk, Ledger Recover, SLIP-39, Apple, Google, Signal, WhatsApp, Bitwarden, 1Password, LastPass, and Midnight's own Passport ([component C14](https://github.com/midnightntwrk/passport/blob/main/docs/plans/components/C14-total-loss-recovery-flow.md)). None of them combines guardians who stay hidden even while approving, a recovery the circuit proves correct, a veto no guardian holds, and a recovering phone that needs no wallet. From the same reading we ranked what users expect and Lantern lacked. The ranking is our judgement, not a survey:

| 2 | rate-limited recovery requests | rare | **§1, v2** |

It also named five changes that would move the whole field, none of which any product it studied offers with hidden guardians: an emergency lock with the veto card (§2), private inheritance (§3), private proofs that guardians are still there (§4), a hidden professional guardian (§5), and Lantern as a recovery profile inside Midnight Passport (§6). §1 to §4 need the contract, and are what the v2 contract implements. §5 needs no contract change at all. §6 needs a delegation-safe gate first, which is roadmap work outside the branch.

Beneath all four, v2's two shared trees, the guardian leaves and the lineage, are 32 levels deep, not v1's 20: 2^32 leaves each, not 2^20, which puts filling them out of practical reach (v1's limit, [SECURITY.md §6](../SECURITY.md#6-known-limitations)); that adds 372 rows to every circuit with a membership proof. ## 1. Rate-limited opens

**The threat.** In v1, `openRecovery` is permissionless: the recovering phone has lost everything, so it holds no secret to authenticate with. One fee buys one more recovery the owner must veto within 72 hours, and an owner who is offline for three days loses ([SECURITY.md §6.4](../SECURITY.md#6-known-limitations)). Every open is also a public question, "is the owner still watching?", which anyone can ask as often as they pay: the liveness oracle, the design's largest privacy cost ([SECURITY.md §5](../SECURITY.md#5-leakage)). The research ranked rate-limited requests second of the ten things users expect. This is the largest gap between Lantern and a production system.

**The rule.**
- **One open recovery per identity root.** A new open is refused while the last one can still finalize.
- **Only a current guardian can open.** The opener proves membership in the identity's current guardian set, with the same proof an approval uses; opening is not approving, and the count starts at zero. The recovering phone still holds no secret: it asks a guardian to open for it, which is what v1's own story already does.
- **At most one open per guardian, per identity, per quarter** (a fixed 13-week period), enforced by an open nullifier. A successful recovery gives every guardian a fresh quota for the successor.
- **A doubling cooldown after vetoes.** After a veto the next open waits 1 day; then 2, 4, 8 and 16 days, and never more than 32. A rotation of the guardian set resets the count, since it evicts whoever was opening. The new set's context is derived from the identity root, `guardianCtxOf(idRoot, seed)`, so nobody watching pending transactions can take it first.
- **The owner's reserved recovery.** A veto may name the owner's next device; that one recovery may open during the cooldown, so the cooldown's end is never a race. Without it, *t* colluding guardians could open again at the public second each cooldown ends, and keep an owner who holds only the card out for good: the one high finding of [the review](#what-the-adversarial-review-changed).
- **A veto counts only against the current guardian set.** A veto of a recovery from a set that has since been rotated out still cancels it, but cannot bring back the cooldown the rotation reset. A veto brackets its time with its own 1-hour slack, so a slow proof does not fail on replay.
- **Expiry.** A recovery that misses its quorum within 7 days of opening, or is not finalized within 7 days of its earliest finalize time, stops holding the slot and can never finalize. So at most one recovery per identity can ever still finalize.
- **The timeline is frozen into the record** at the open: the earliest finalize time, the approval deadline and the expiry. A watcher shows the exact times without knowing any constant.

```
ledger liveRecovery:   Map<Bytes<32>, Bytes<32>>;   // idRoot -> the latest open
ledger openNullifiers: Set<Bytes<32>>;              // H(guardian secret, head, quarter)
ledger vetoCounts:     Map<Bytes<32>, Counter>;     // idRoot -> vetoes; reset by rotation
ledger lastVetoAt:     Map<Bytes<32>, Uint<64>>;    // idRoot -> the later time bound of the last veto
ledger reservedRecovery: Map<Bytes<32>, Bytes<32>>; // idRoot -> the one recovery a veto exempted

circuit openRecovery(idCommit, ephemeralPk, quarter)
  assert the quarter is the current one                    // two block-time checks
  assert the caller's leaf is in the current guardian set  // as approveRecovery
  assert !openNullifiers.member(H(secret, idCommit, quarter)), "guardian already opened a recovery this period"
  assert the recovery in liveRecovery[idRoot] is dead, "a recovery is already live for this identity"
  assert vetoCounts[idRoot] == 0 || blockTime >= lastVetoAt[idRoot] + cooldownOf(vetoCounts[idRoot])
         || rid == reservedRecovery[idRoot], "cooling down after a veto"
  record { ..., unlockAt = hi + delay, approveBy = hi + 7 days, expiresAt = unlockAt + 7 days }

circuit vetoRecovery(rid, nextRid)                         // nextRid = recoveryIdOf(head, next device key)
  as v1, then, only if the recovery's guardian set is still the current one:
  vetoCounts[idRoot] += 1;  lastVetoAt[idRoot] = hi;  reservedRecovery[idRoot] = nextRid

dead(R) = vetoed || finalized || rotated out || past expiresAt || (past approveBy && below quorum)
```

**Why a guardian must open.** "One open recovery per identity" on top of a permissionless open creates a worse attack than the one it fixes. A stranger opens a recovery for their own device. It can never reach quorum, but it holds the owner's only slot; when it dies, the stranger's script wins the race to open again. The owner's real recovery never opens, and v1's attrition surface has become an availability failure. Every fix that keeps opens permissionless either brings back unlimited parallel opens or lets a newer open displace an older one, which would let anyone displace the owner's recovery too. What the squatter lacks is guardian membership, so v2 asks for it. Three other options were rejected:
- **a bond**, because Compact 0.31.1 has no value transfer usable here without a wallet-bound primitive, which [`test/authentication.test.js`](../test/authentication.test.js) forbids;
- **an open authorised by the owner's identity secret**, because an owner who has lost the device has no identity secret;
- **an open authorised by the veto card**, because the card is kept for defence only: it cancels, and in v2 it locks. A card that could also open would let whoever copied it hold the slot.

**Privacy.** The liveness oracle shrinks from "anyone, as often as they pay" to "the identity's own guardians, once per identity per quarter each, with doubling waits after each veto". It does not disappear: a guardian can still ask. An opener is one of *n* guardians (a count, not a list, which `addGuardian` history already reveals), exactly like an approver, and the open nullifier cannot be linked across quarters or to the same guardian's approvals and check-ins. The slot and the veto count can already be derived in v1 from `recoveries`, `killed` and transaction times. A reservation is new and public: a non-zero one says the owner vetoed from somewhere other than the device being recovered, which suggests they lost it, and when the reserved recovery opens, everyone knows it is the owner's own. It names a hash of the head and a device key, never a guardian, and a veto that reserves nothing publishes nothing new.

**What it costs.**
- `openRecovery` grows from 4,657 rows to 13,915, from k = 13 to k = 14, because it now carries a membership proof and checks the reservation. `vetoRecovery` grows by 524 rows and `rotateGuardianSet` by 448.
- An owner whose guardians cannot be reached cannot even start a recovery. In v1 they could not finish one either, so this moves the dependency earlier rather than adding one.
- A mistaken open, for example with the wrong device key, uses that guardian's quarter and holds the slot until its approval deadline. The owner's veto frees it sooner, at the price of a 1-day cooldown.
- **A hostile guardian can hold the slot.** A guardian working against the owner, without enough others to reach quorum, can open a recovery that never will. It holds the slot until its approval deadline, 7 days after the open, and that guardian cannot open again for the same identity until the next quarter: at most 7 days per hostile guardian per quarter, or 14 when two opens straddle a quarter boundary. v1 had no such single-guardian delay. As with a mistaken open, the owner's veto frees the slot sooner.
- **Opens should be sponsored.** In v1 anyone can open, so the wallet that pays for an open says nothing about guardianship; in v1's story the opener paid from her own wallet (the README's step 3.2). In v2 an open proves that its caller is a current guardian, so a guardian who pays from their own wallet ties that wallet to the open. v2's opens should be paid by someone else, a sponsor, as the approvals are paid by an operator wallet in the recorded runs. The contract's own rate limit then meets the sponsor rule in [SECURITY.md §4.2b](../SECURITY.md#42b-b2--the-fee-sponsor): never pay for opens except under a per-identity rate limit.
- It changes a v1 rule: a v1 client that lets the phone open for itself does not work against v2.

## 2. An emergency lock with the veto card

**The threat.** Whoever holds the identity secret (a thief with the lost laptop, malware, or *t* guardians who pooled their shares) can act as the owner at every DApp until the owner's recovery finalizes. In v1 that window is at least 72 hours, and it is the honest cost of any recovery scheme ([SECURITY.md §4.5](../SECURITY.md#45-e--anyone-holding-the-identity-secret-but-not-the-veto-card), [§4.3](../SECURITY.md#43-c--t-colluding-guardians)). The research named an emergency lock held by the veto card as one of five changes that would move the field: a card freeze for a private identity.

**The rule.**
- The veto card locks the identity, across its whole lineage, in one transaction. The card is kept apart from the device, so it is exactly what a thief with the device lacks.
- While it is locked, the gate (`hostGatedAction`) and `proveHeadOwnership`, v1's "I currently control this root", refuse with "identity is locked". The stolen secret stops working at every DApp that reads Lantern's ledger.
- Everything the owner needs still works while locked: veto, rotation, adding a guardian, opening, approving and finalizing a recovery, and check-ins. Lock, then recover, is the point.
- Lifting the lock needs the identity secret **and** the veto card, and installs a new veto card if the owner gives one. A thief with the device cannot lift it. Someone who copied the card but not the secret can lock (a nuisance) but cannot lift it, and once the owner lifts the lock and replaces the card, cannot lock again.
- A finalize to a new device clears the lock, and the successor starts unlocked.

```
ledger locked: Map<Bytes<32>, Boolean>;             // idRoot -> locked; seeded false at enrolment

circuit lockIdentity(idCommit)                      // enrolled, not retired
  assert vetoCommits.lookup(idCommit) == vetoCommitOf(vetoSecret(), vetoSalt()),
         "locking requires the veto secret"
  locked.insert(idRoots.lookup(idCommit), true)

circuit unlockIdentity(idCommit, newVetoCommit)     // enrolled, not retired
  assert idCommitOf(identitySecret(), idSalt()) == idCommit, "not the identity owner"
  assert vetoCommits.lookup(idCommit) == vetoCommitOf(vetoSecret(), vetoSalt()),
         "unlocking requires the veto secret"
  locked.insert(idRoots.lookup(idCommit), false)
  vetoCommits.insert(idCommit, newVetoCommit)       // the current commitment keeps the card

hostGatedAction, proveHeadOwnership:  assert !locked.lookup(idRoot), "identity is locked"
finalizeRecovery:                     locked.insert(rec.idRoot, false)
```

`ownergate.compact` stays unchanged: the lock is one more assert beside its predicate, with its own message. A retired commitment's card can neither lock nor lift the lock, so an old card never reaches the successor. An independently deployed DApp reads a committee-signed snapshot, not Lantern's ledger; a v2 snapshot would have to leave locked roots out, and that is not built ([`src/host/snapshot.js`](../src/host/snapshot.js) is v1's).

**Privacy.** One public bit per identity root. A lock says "this owner believes a secret is exposed", and also proves that the owner is alive and holds the card: a proof of life, but a voluntary one, unlike the liveness oracle. It says nothing about the guardians.

**What it costs.**
- `lockIdentity` is 4,812 rows (k = 13) and `unlockIdentity` 9,586 (k = 14). The gate grows by 377 rows: 5 for the lock, and 372 because v2's trees are 32 levels deep.
- A copied veto card is worth more to a thief than in v1. It could already cancel every recovery; now it can also lock the identity at DApps and push up the cooldown. Unlocking, which needs both secrets and works whether or not the identity is locked, also installs a new veto card, so a copied card stops working.

## 3. A delay chosen at enrolment, and an inheritance profile

**The need.** One 72-hour delay does not fit everyone. It is short for someone travelling or in hospital, and it rules out the one recovery people plan for years ahead: inheritance. The research ranked a delay the owner chooses eighth. Inheritance tools exist outside crypto (Apple's Legacy Contact, Google's Inactive Account Manager) and inside it (Casa's and Nunchuk's inheritance plans). In each, a company holds the plan, and usually learns who the heirs are.

**The rule.**
- `enrollIdentity` takes a delay from 24 hours to 90 days (86,400 to 7,776,000 seconds), and refuses anything outside.
- It is stored once per identity root, so every successor inherits it with no copy, as it inherits the guardians.
- Each open freezes it into the record as the earliest finalize time, and `finalizeRecovery` checks that. The lock still runs from the later of the two block-time bounds recorded at the open, so a lying prover can shave at most the 10-minute slack, and never below the delay, as in v1. The client keeps 72 hours as its default.
- It is fixed for the life of the lineage: no circuit takes a delay after enrolment, so a thief with the laptop can never shorten it.

```
ledger recoveryDelays: Map<Bytes<32>, Uint<64>>;     // idRoot -> seconds, written once

circuit enrollIdentity(idCommit, vetoCommit, threshold, delay)   // as in v1, plus:
  assert delay >= 86400, "delay below the minimum"
  assert delay <= 7776000, "delay above the maximum"
  recoveryDelays.insert(idCommit, delay)                         // the genesis root
```

**The inheritance profile: a private dead man's switch.** The owner enrols with a 90-day delay; the guardians are the heirs, or an executor and the heirs. The owner runs a watcher, and the guardians check in each quarter (§4). Today's watchers, "Watch an identity" on [/live](https://lantern-midnight.vercel.app/live) and `npm run watch`, follow v1's recorded contracts; a v2 watcher would read v2's. While the owner is alive, any open is visible for 90 days and the veto card cancels it. After the owner's death, a guardian opens a recovery for the executor's device, *t* guardians approve within 7 days, nobody vetoes, and after 90 days the device finalizes and proves the secret is the right one. Every DApp that gates on the identity root keeps working for the heir. No heir is named anywhere: a private will with a public, provable execution.

**Privacy.** The delay is public per identity root, so a 90-day delay marks an identity as set up for inheritance. Hiding it would need a committed delay and an in-circuit range check against block time.

**What it costs.**
- `enrollIdentity` grows from 7,529 rows to 7,673, still k = 13.
- The delay is lineage-wide, so the owner's own recovery after an ordinary lost phone also waits 90 days. A later change that needs both secrets and applies only to recoveries opened after it is a follow-up.
- An owner offline for longer than the delay loses the race with a hostile recovery, as with any timelock.

## 4. Private guardian check-ins

**The need.** Shares get lost, guardians drift away, and in v1 the owner finds out when a recovery fails. The research ranked guardian health checks third: Casa asks every six months, DeRec builds them into its protocol, and Apple has its own version for recovery contacts. Every one of those checks reveals who the guardians are to whoever runs it. Lantern cannot ask its guardians in public without naming them.

**The rule.**
- Once per quarter, each guardian proves membership in the identity's current guardian set, with the proof `approveRecovery` uses, and writes a nullifier bound to their secret, the guardian context and the quarter. A guardian with two leaves still counts once.
- Each check-in adds one to a public counter for that identity root, context and quarter. The owner reads "2 of 3 guardians checked in this quarter", and the record never says which two.
- The counter is keyed by the context, so a rotation starts a fresh count for the new set; a recovery keeps the context, so the count carries across a finalize.
- Every guardian checks in against the identity root, never a later commitment of the lineage, and the circuit refuses anything else. Otherwise the choice between the root and the current head could tell one guardian's client from another.

```
ledger checkIns:          Map<Bytes<32>, Counter>;  // H(idRoot, ctx, quarter) -> guardians who checked in
ledger checkInNullifiers: Set<Bytes<32>>;           // H(guardian secret, ctx, quarter)

circuit checkIn(idRoot, quarter)
  assert idRoots.lookup(idRoot) == idRoot, "check in with the identity root"
  assert the quarter is the current one
  assert the caller's leaf is in the current guardian set  // leaf bound before checkRoot
  assert !checkInNullifiers.member(nul), "guardian already checked in this period"
  checkInNullifiers.insert(nul)
  checkIns[H(idRoot, ctx, quarter)].increment(1)
```

**Privacy.** Not public: which guardian. The nullifier changes with the quarter and the context, so one guardian's check-ins cannot be linked across quarters, nor to their approvals or opens, which use other domains and inputs. Public: the count, that some current guardian was active at that block, and which historic guardian root the proof used, the same metadata as an approval ([SECURITY.md §5](../SECURITY.md#5-leakage)). Timing still narrows: a guardian who checks in minutes after a reminder is easier to place, and an owner who reminds guardians one at a time and watches the counter could learn who checked in. So the v2 client never checks in at once: it picks a random moment within the next 7 days, inside the quarter. And the count itself is a new signal: it shows which identities have inattentive guardians, a hint for anyone choosing a target. That is the price of a public health check.

**What it proves, exactly.** That a guardian still holds their token: the guardian secret and leaf salt from their kit, with a valid path. It does not prove they still hold the Shamir share, because the leaf does not commit to the share. The kit keeps the two together; a later leaf format could bind a commitment to the share itself.

**What it costs.** `checkIn` is 12,583 rows (k = 14), about the size of an approval, and each check-in is one transaction per guardian per quarter. A sponsor should pay for it, for the reason opens should be sponsored (§1): a guardian's own wallet would tie it to the check-in.

## 5. A hidden professional guardian

**The need.** Not everyone has *t* people they trust with a share. Custodial recovery services answer this today (Casa holds a recovery key; Ledger Recover splits a seed across companies), and each is a publicly known party that can be phished, pressured or subpoenaed as the obvious guardian.

**The design.** A service is one guardian among *n*: it holds one share and one guardian token, and its leaf is a salted commitment like every other. It approves only after its own check, for example a video call against details the owner left with it at enrolment, and it compares the fingerprint words aloud like any guardian. Because its leaf looks like any other, nobody reading the chain can tell whether an identity uses a service at all, or which one. With *t* ≥ 2 the service alone learns nothing about the secret.

**Why no contract change.** It works on v1 today. What is missing is operational: the service's approval flow, its check, sponsored fees, and terms. It fits the distribution in the README: an SDK, plus a hosted sponsor and watcher, plus an optional guardian.

**Privacy and cost.** The service knows its own customers, as any custodian does, but that list never reaches the public record. The owner trusts it with one share and with a fair check before approving; the threshold and the veto card bound what a dishonest service can do alone.

## 6. Lantern as a Midnight Passport C14 profile

**The need.** Midnight's Passport project decided in 2026/07 to use stateless guardians with paper keys (BUSS, ANARKey) for total-loss recovery ([C14](https://github.com/midnightntwrk/passport/blob/main/docs/plans/components/C14-total-loss-recovery-flow.md)). Lantern is a different point in the same space: a guardian set that is enforced on chain and stays hidden, public notice and a veto no guardian holds, and a recovery the circuit proves correct. We set out the comparison as prior art on [Passport issue #20](https://github.com/midnightntwrk/passport/issues/20#issuecomment-5821240645) ([the note](upstream/passport-c14-prior-art.md)), as a data point and not a request to reopen the decision.

**The design.** Passport keeps its default. An owner who wants the rules enforced on chain chooses Lantern as a second recovery profile; Passport's credentials gate on the Lantern identity root, as any DApp does, so a recovery changes nothing Passport stores.

**What stands in the way.** Every Lantern owner and guardian gate takes its secret as a witness, so a remote prover learns it: the construction Passport's recovery draft rejects under REC-11. A delegation-safe gate built on signatures ([Roadmap 4](../README.md#roadmap)) comes first. That is the real cost of this profile, and it is not in v2.

## What the adversarial review changed

An adversarial review of the first v2 build ran each attack below as a script, assuming the attacker wins every race. All eight findings were valid, and each is fixed, with a test that runs the attack and expects it to fail. The rules above already include the fixes.

| | Finding | Fix | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| F0, high | *t* colluding guardians could open again at the public second each cooldown ended, and keep an owner who holds only the card out for good | a veto reserves the owner's next recovery, which may open during the cooldown | §1 |
| F1, medium | anyone watching pending transactions could take a rotation's or an enrolment's new context first | the context is derived from the identity root | §1 |
| F2, medium-low | a veto could bring back a cooldown that a rotation had reset | a veto counts only against the current guardian set | §1 |
| F3, medium | whoever copied the card could keep the owner locked out for ever | unlocking installs a new card | §2 |
| F4, low | a veto proof went stale 10 minutes after its claimed time | vetoes get a 1-hour slack | §1 |
| F5, informational | the shared trees could be filled; inherited from v1 | 32-level trees | [At a glance](#at-a-glance) |
| F6, low | the client disagreed with the contract just after a rotation | a client fix | — |
| F7, low, privacy | check-ins show a guardian's fee payer beside the identity every quarter, and reminders can unmask who checked in | a random check-in moment in the client; the fee should come from a sponsor | §4 |
| F8, low, privacy | the check-in argument could tell one guardian's client from another | check in with the identity root | §4 |

## What v2 does not change

- The shipped contract on Preprod is frozen and unchanged, and so are its records, the whole-story records, the story's 74 steps and the site's `/demo`.
- `identity.compact` and `ownergate.compact` are imported, not edited.
